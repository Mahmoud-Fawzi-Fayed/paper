\abstract{
Federated Learning (FL) is a distributed learning framework that enables clients to jointly optimize a common model through server coordination, while their local training data remain within their respective environments. Despite its privacy advantages, FL encounters significant challenges resulting from the participation of numerous clients having heterogeneous data during each training round. Consequently, this degrades accuracy, delays convergence, increases the communication overhead, and undermines fairness among participating clients. Hence, Client Selection (CS) has become essential for identifying the most beneficial clients for global model training. However, existing approaches often struggle to preserve model accuracy and fairness while limiting communication costs in the presence of non-Independent and Identically Distributed (non-IID) data. In response to these challenges, we introduce FedFACS (Federated Fair Adaptive Client Selection), a novel framework designed to handle data heterogeneity while improving fairness and training efficiency. FedFACS formulates client selection using a multi-objective fitness function combining the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between local and global predictions with a dynamic fairness baseline. In addition, we incorporate an uncertainty-aware penalty that mitigates the impact of local noise. Moreover, a control mechanism is introduced to adaptively balance utility and fairness in response to global convergence feedback. A dynamic cohort sizing mechanism was developed that identifies the optimal number of high-fitness clients needed for each round. Furthermore, we propose a nonnegative normalised aggregation family whose default, the sample-count weighting, keeps the aggregate consistent with the population objective, and we compare it with stability weighting. Extensive experiments across multiple datasets under several data heterogeneous settings show that FedFACS achieves average improvements of 1.39\% in accuracy, 1.41\% in F1-score, and 0.44\% in recall, as well as 3.47\% fewer selected clients while maintaining comparable communication rounds, in addition to improving fairness by an average of 1.38\% in Jain's Fairness Index (JFI), relative to the best performing existing method, thereby enhancing performance and reducing communication overhead while ensuring fairness.
}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec1}
Federated Learning (FL) establishes a distributed collaborative framework, where multiple devices are coordinated by a central server for joint model optimization, while each participant performs local training and maintains its data within its own environment throughout the training process, thus maintaining data privacy while reducing communication overhead. Consequently, FL has gained considerable attention in applications where data confidentiality is critical, including smart transportation, financial services, and healthcare (Shanmugarasa et al. 2023). 

In conventional federated learning, model training is organized into successive communication rounds coordinated by the server. At the beginning of each round, the latest global model is transmitted to all participating clients, which use the received model to conduct local training and subsequently return their updates to the coordinating server for aggregation. This cycle continues over successive rounds until the specified training accuracy threshold is reached (Kairouz et al. 2021), as depicted in Fig. 1. A major concern associated with FL is the communication overhead resulting from the repeated exchange of model parameters among participating clients and the coordinating server across successive training rounds. This overhead tends to increase as more clients join the federated network.

\caption{Federated learning iterative process.}\label{fig:fl_arch}
\end{figure}

Moreover, the training process becomes more challenging when participating clients have different data distributions, dataset sizes, data quality, or computational capabilities. Such heterogeneity may introduce fairness issues, slow global convergence, and negatively impact the performance of the resulting global model (Kairouz et al. 2021; Khajehali et al. 2023; Mayhoub and Shami 2024; Fu et al. 2023; Wen et al. 2023).

Consequently, an effective client selection strategy is vital for identifying the most informative participants in each round contributes toward global model improvement, thereby limiting communication overhead (Asad et al. 2023), accelerating convergence, and enhancing performance. However, several client selection techniques rely on predefined participation ratios or fixed cohort sizes (Lai et al. 2021; Larsson et al. 2021). Such methods can result in the selection of participants that provide limited contributions to the global model. Other approaches are categorized as resource-based (Nishio and Yonetani 2019; Li et al. 2022), bandwidth-based (Ji et al. 2023), or correlation-based (Cho et al. 2022; Tang et al. 2021). However, most of these techniques fail to balance global and local factors, neglecting fair participation under non-IID data distributions. As a result, participants with larger datasets, better data quality, or stronger computational resources are repeatedly selected, while others remain idle for many rounds, which can bias the global model and affect generalization (Vucinich and Zhu 2023; Ayeelyan et al. 2025). Nonetheless, balancing performance and fairness in FL client selection under non-IID conditions is still an open challenge.

To overcome this limitation, we propose the Federated Fair Adaptive Client Selection (FedFACS) framework to harmonize the conflicts between fairness with predictive performance, communication efficiency, and convergence under diverse non-IID scenarios. FedFACS introduces a multi-dimensional selection fitness score that prioritizes clients offering the highest contribution to global model by leveraging three core factors: utility, fairness, and uncertainty penalty. Client utility is quantified by estimating its potential contribution to the global model through the KL divergence between local and global predictive distributions, which measures how far the local model has moved from the global model (Kullback and Leibler 1951). The uncertainty penalty is estimated using the variance of KL divergence measured across multiple mini-batches for each client. Fairness is quantified using normalized idle rounds, dynamically determined by the server, thereby reducing client domination (Shi et al. 2024; Xu et al. 2024). Moreover, FedFACS balances utility and fairness using a bounded feedback control mechanism. The number of clients selected for aggregation (cohort size) is adaptively updated at every round in response to the current global model loss. Furthermore, the client contributions are quantified by a stability score that defines an optional weighting of the global aggregation, which is evaluated against sample-count weights. The efficacy of FedFACS is demonstrated through rigorous evaluations across four benchmark datasets, outperforming competitive FL baselines across different data distribution settings. The key contributions of the proposed approach are summarized as follows:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*-]
\item Multi-Objective Fitness Function for Fair Adaptive Client Selection: We propose an adaptive client selection fitness function integrating KL-divergence-based client utility, an uncertainty penalty to improve robustness, and a dynamic fairness score for fair participation. Furthermore, the convergence progress of the global federated model guides the adjustment of fitness scores and determines the cohort size, thereby specifying the number of top-ranked clients selected for model aggregation in each training round. Specifically, the cohort size increases during convergence stagnation to encourage exploration and decreases during stable convergence under non-IID data settings to improve communication efficiency.

\item Adaptive control of the utility and fairness: We introduce a convergence-aware control parameter that dynamically balances utility and fairness for each client during training rounds according to the convergence behavior of the global model. Thus, the framework adjusts the relative weighting of fairness and utility based on the current convergence state.

\item Normalised aggregation weights: we present a nonnegative weight family $S_i^t=(1-\kappa)\,n_i/\sum_j n_j+\kappa\,q_i^t$ that contains the sample-count weighting of the population objective ($\kappa=0$) and the stability weighting ($\kappa=1$) and is defined for negative scores and identical client models; the comparison of the two, reported in Section 5.4, selects $\kappa=0$, which gives the more stable global model at the end of training.

\item We perform experimental evaluation using four benchmark datasets (MNIST, FMNIST, CIFAR10, and COVID-19) across diverse data heterogeneity conditions. The evaluation demonstrates improvements over existing federated learning methods in accuracy, communication efficiency, and fairness.
\end{enumerate}




\section{Related Works}
\label{sec2}

This section discusses prior work in federated learning, which addresses client selection, communication efficient optimization, aggregation, and fairness-aware techniques.

\subsection{Classical Federated Learning}

Over recent years, FL has gained considerable attention across numerous areas, such as healthcare, edge computing, finance, and computer vision (Ahmed and Alabi 2024; Darzi et al. 2024; Khan et al. 2024). In computer vision domain, FL has shown effectiveness across multiple tasks, including image classification and recognition. FL faces two major challenges which are data heterogeneity and communication overhead. These challenges affect model performance. Consequently, there is a critical need for developing an optimized FL framework to overcome these challenges.

FedAvg acts as a simple and communication-efficient baseline for FL by averaging updates collected from participating clients to construct a shared global model. However, FedAvg struggles under non-IID data, which degrades performance and biases the global model. In addition, it ignores fairness (McMahan et al. 2017). Li et al. (2020) presented FedProx, which utilizes a proximal term to ensure stable local training and support faster convergence across clients with heterogeneous data distributions, although it introduces additional computational overhead. The SCAFFOLD approach proposed by Karimireddy et al. (2020) accelerates convergence through control variates that reduce the drift between server and client parameter updates. Nevertheless, it increases communication overhead as well as failing under highly heterogeneous data conditions, while FedAdam (Reddi et al. 2021) is an adaptive optimization approach that utilizes an adaptive server-side optimizer to enhance convergence. However, it raises server-side computation cost and does not address the fairness issue. Furthermore, FedNova (Wang et al. 2020) improves the convergence rate by eliminating objective inconsistency through normalizing local updates before aggregation, which enhances fairness. However, it does not address the impact of a large number of participants on communication cost under non-IID scenarios. 

\subsection{Modern Client Selection and Model Aggregation}

Classical federated learning assumes that each client possesses datasets of identical data and quality, as well as homogeneous hardware and network resources. Practically, participants have heterogeneous systems and data, which introduces many challenges, including performance degradation affecting the global model's accuracy and convergence rate. Moreover, for scalability and communication-efficiency, transmitting updates from all clients in every training round is impractical.


Numerous studies have proposed client selection methods, including random, communication-based, utility-based, correlation-based, and resource-based selection (Ayeelyan et al. 2025). In random selection methods, a fixed client count is chosen in each training round. However, these approaches suffer from restricted adaptability, inefficient convergence, and a lack of heterogeneous data awareness. Communication-based client selection methods aim to reduce latency and communication overhead. Li et al. (2024) proposed a performance-aware weight decay mechanism that utilizes historical model performance to adaptively control the number of participants, enhancing global model accuracy and decreasing communication costs. Tsouvalas et al. (2024) introduced a communication efficient algorithm named FedCompress, which enhances communication-efficiency by compressing the model while preserving model accuracy through dynamic clustered weighting. However, its beneficial effects may be restricted under strongly heterogeneous data distribution. Vanthang et al. (2025) proposed a utility-based client sampling framework that integrates fog computing with federated learning. The framework selects the most informative clients according to their gradients and reduces data transmission by adaptively adjusting selection thresholds under data heterogeneous scenario. Lai et al. (2021) developed the Oort client selection framework, which computes a utility score defined by three key factors: training time, local loss, and the client's past selection count. Cho et al. (2022) addressed communication efficiency through the Power-of-Choice strategy by prioritizing clients with high loss in every training round to accelerate global convergence. Ye et al. (2025) proposed an attention-based selection approach that enhances accuracy while minimizing communication overhead by adopting a “later-is-better” concept, where the client selection process ranks participants using their estimated contribution together with their alignment with the global model during each communication round. Zhai et al. (2024) developed an efficient approach for client selection that selects participants according to their evaluated quality. This approach employs a Multi-Armed Bandit (MAB) metric to evaluate client quality using two factors: individual contribution and correlation across clients. Consequently, the method accelerates convergence while enhancing global model accuracy. DivFL (Balakrishnan et al. 2022) is correlation-based selection method that improves convergence speed and performance by choosing the clients according to their gradient information. Nishio and Yonetani (2019) introduced a resource-based approach for selecting clients, considering clients’ data size, bandwidth, computational and communication capabilities to estimate client’s training time. To improve convergence, the server selects clients that are able to transmit their updates within a specified time. Liu et al. (2025) introduced AHIBCS, an approach designed to improve FL performance when data is non-identically distributed by leveraging both current and historical client behavior. The proposed method considers both the current training status and historical information of clients through an adaptive threshold mechanism. Based on the variation of local loss values, the algorithm dynamically switches between two client selection strategies: a loss-based strategy that prioritizes clients with higher losses to accelerate convergence, and a diversity-based strategy that selects clients with more diverse model updates to improve model generalization.

Model aggregation is performed after each communication round, where the selected client updates are aggregated at the server to produce the global model. Classical federated aggregation methods determine client weights according to their local dataset sizes (Ayeelyan et al. 2025; Zhai et al. 2024). The approach proposed by Ji et al. (2019) utilized trainable attention scores to weight local updates during global model aggregation. Furthermore, Wu et al. (2021) quantified client utility by measuring the directional alignment of local update vectors with the global optimization direction. These quantified values determine the weights assigned to each local update during every aggregation round. However, both approaches in (Ji et al. 2019; Wu et al. 2021) introduced an additional computational complexity and suppress rare informative features from clients possessing heterogeneous data. Zhai et al. (2024) introduced a dynamic aggregation method, which adaptively assigns weights according to the quality of the selected participants to improve model robustness and convergence.  


\subsection{Fairness-Based Client Selection}

Fairness in FL should aim to provide all clients with a fair opportunity to contribute during training. (Huang et al. 2024). Under non-IID conditions, a challenge arises from over exploiting high-contributing clients while under exploring underrepresented clients, leading to a biased global model that favors the repeatedly selected clients. Moreover, equal clients’ participation frequency affects the global model performance. Therefore, adaptive fairness mechanisms should be developed to balance exploitation (training with well-performing clients) and exploration (training with low-performing clients) to ensure fair participation (Bouzamoucha et al. 2025; Mukhtiar 2025; Mukhtiar et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2022). Numerous approaches address fairness in federated learning from different perspectives.  


Huang et al. (2020) ensured fairness by selecting suitable clients utilizing a client selection method. However, fairness was not guaranteed, as the approach quantifies random selection probability. Song et al. (2022) developed a fairness-constrained reputation method to achieve a balance between client participation frequency and reputation in FL. Chaudhury et al. (2024) proposed a veto core approach to prevent client domination, preventing group deviation. However, this technique introduces significant communication and computational burdens. Wang et al. (2023) developed an entropy-based framework that balances fairness with global model accuracy. Shi et al. (2023) introduced a Lyapunov-based client selection approach, where the selection score depends on client reputation, participation frequency and Shapley value. Moreover, this approach allows clients to redeem their reputation to ensure fair participation and adjust their selection scores. However, it comes with computational overhead. Although multiple studies in FL have demonstrated the benefits of fair client participation, mainly in balancing exploitation and exploration under non-IID environments. Nevertheless, the problem of balancing fairness and global model performance while reducing communication cost remains open.

Existing federated learning approaches optimize accuracy, fairness, or communication efficiency separately, lacking an adaptive mechanism to balance these objectives based on the global convergence state, resulting in imbalanced performance, high communication overhead and participant bias under heterogeneous data distributions.

To address these limitations, an efficient client selection strategy is required to identify suitable clients for aggregation. Our proposed framework improves accuracy and scalability while minimizing communication overhead through fewer communication rounds and adaptive client cohort selection in each round. Furthermore, FedFACS integrates a suitable fairness mechanism to prevent the dominance of high-quality participants, prevent bias in the global model, and ensure fair client participation. It also incorporates an adaptive weighted aggregation method to maintain robustness and enhance performance under data heterogeneity distributions, addressing the limitations identified by previous studies.

\section{Problem Formulation }\label{sec3}
% REVISED (Replacement 1): objective renamed J(w), complete selection rule, single aggregation equation, indices fixed
The federated learning paradigm relies on a centralized server to coordinate a population of $N$ participating clients.

In federated learning, distributed clients independently optimize their local models using private datasets, while the server coordinates these local updates to develop a unified global model without transferring clients' original data. Training aggregates participants' updates through weighted averaging so as to minimize the objective below.

Let $\mathcal{C}=\{1,\dots,N\}$ be the client population, $D_i$ the dataset of client $i$, $n_i=|D_i|>0$, and $n=\sum_i n_i$, with $\ell$ the per-example loss and $(x_{ij},y_{ij})$ the $j$-th labeled example of client $i$. Data across clients are non-IID, so dataset size, quality, and distribution vary across clients. The learning objective over the model parameters $w$ and the local empirical loss are

\begin{equation}
J(w) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{n_i}{n}\, f_i(w)
\label{eq:global_obj}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
f_i(w) = \frac{1}{n_i}\sum_{j=1}^{n_i} \ell(w; x_{ij}, y_{ij})
\label{eq:local_obj}
\end{equation}

In practical federated learning, several challenges arise as data heterogeneity leads to biased, unstable, and noisy updates. Moreover, selecting all clients every training round is impractical due to communication overhead. In addition, unfair client selection leads to repeatedly choosing high-quality clients, which biases the global model. All of these issues negatively affect the overall performance.

At round $t$, the server selects a cohort $C_k^t \subseteq \mathcal{C}$ of size $m_t$ according to the fitness score of each client (Eq.~(14)). The cohort aims to maximize the total fitness, with ties broken by ascending client identifier, as in Eq.~(3), where $A$ is a candidate subset of $\mathcal{C}$, subject to the waiting-time guard of Section~\ref{sec:properties}. The terms of the fitness score are defined in Section 4.2.

\begin{equation}
C_k^t \in \arg\max_{\substack{A \subseteq \mathcal{C} \\ |A| = m_t}} \sum_{i \in A} \mathrm{Fitness}_i^t
\label{eq:client_selection}
\end{equation}

To reduce upload traffic, the cohort size $m_t=|C_k^t|$ is adjusted dynamically from the convergence efficiency $\rho^{t-1}$ of the previous round (Eq.~(16)) and is bounded by $C_{k_{\min}} \le m_t \le C_{k_{\max}}$, where $C_{k_{\min}}$ and $C_{k_{\max}}$ are the lower and upper bounds, respectively.

The next global model is the weighted average of the trained local models $w_i^t$ with nonnegative weights $S_i^t$ that sum to one (Eq.~(18)), as shown in Eq.~(4). Equation (1) is the learning objective. The ranking of Eq.~(3) decides which clients take part in a round, and the aggregation with $\kappa=0$ weights the selected clients by their sample counts, which is the weighting of Eq.~(1) restricted to the selected clients; the stability weights ($\kappa>0$) form a variance-aware alternative that Section~\ref{sec:score_analysis} evaluates.

\begin{equation}
w_{\text{global}}^{t+1} = \sum_{i \in C_k^t} S_i^t\, w_i^t
\label{eq:global_update}
\end{equation}


\section{The Proposed Approach}\label{sec4}

This section outlines the proposed client selection approach, followed by descriptions of the algorithms executed on the client and server sides. Finally, the implementation details are provided.

\subsection{FedFACS Overview}

FedFACS is a novel fair adaptive client selection approach for federated learning. The proposed approach introduces an adaptive and efficient federated learning paradigm which improves communication efficiency, accuracy, and fairness under heterogeneous data scenarios. FedFACS selects the informative clients while ensuring fair selection by balancing utility and fairness based on current global convergence state. Thus, it prevents dominance among high-quality clients and avoids permanently excluding clients with smaller datasets or lower utility, since such clients may still contribute to global model performance and enhance generalization. The proposed framework follows a standard client-server FL architecture. It builds a final generalized efficient global model by operating in an iterative communication rounds between distributed clients and centralized server.

Each training round $t$ proceeds as follows (Fig. 2). (Step 1) The coordinating server broadcasts the committed global model and the control state, namely the deviation signal $\nu^t$ (Eq. (10)) and the mean idle count $\overline{r^t}$ (Eq. (19)), to all $N$ candidate clients. (Step 2) Each candidate resets its local model to the global model, trains it for $E$ local epochs with learning rate $\eta$ on the loss of Eq. (2), and computes its composite fitness score on the same scoring examples, using the unchanged global model as reference. (Step 3) Each client transmits its composite fitness score; the server holds the number of consecutive idle rounds of every client. (Step 4) The server computes the cohort size $m_t$ from the convergence efficiency of the previous round and ranks the clients by fitness. (Step 5) The server selects the top-ranked $m_t$ clients $C_k^t$. (Step 6) The selected clients upload their trained parameters, raw stability score $s_i^t$ (Eq. (13)) and sample count $n_i$.

Finally, the server aggregates the uploaded models with their normalised weights (Eqs. (4) and (18)), validates the result, and updates the global fairness baseline and the control state for the next communication round (Step 7). All candidate clients therefore incur download, local-training and scoring costs in every round, whereas only the selected clients incur the cost of uploading a model update: FedFACS reduces uploads and aggregation contributions, not downlink traffic or local computation. Training proceeds iteratively until the model satisfies the required performance criteria or the specified limit on communication rounds is exhausted.

Algorithm 1 presents the client-side training and fitness evaluation in our proposed framework. The algorithm operates locally on each candidate client to quantify its training effectiveness by computing a composite fitness score. At the beginning of every round, the client resets its local model to the received global model and trains it (Lines 3-8). It then computes the composite fitness score incorporating the KL-divergence score, fairness, and uncertainty penalty on the same scoring examples, with the unchanged global model as reference, while deriving the control parameter \textit{a} to balance utility and fairness according to the convergence state (Lines 11-18). To limit uploads, the client first transmits only its composite fitness score to the server for ranking; the server holds the idle counters and calculates the global fairness baseline $\overline{r^t}$ (Line 19). Finally, if the client is selected, it uploads its local model update, stability score $s_i^t$ and sample count $n_i$ for aggregation (Lines 20-22).

Furthermore, algorithm 2 outlines the overall procedure of server-side client selection and model aggregation. Clients are ranked according to their fitness scores. In addition, the cohort size $m_t$ is dynamically defined from the global convergence efficiency of the previous round, and the cohort of $m_t$ clients is selected, overdue clients first and then those of highest fitness (Lines 3-6). The centralized server receives the trained models, stability scores and sample counts of the selected clients and combines them with the nonnegative stability weights of Eq. (18) to construct an aggregated global model for the subsequent training (Lines 7-8). Furthermore, the resulting candidate model is validated using the validation dataset (Line 9). The model, the feedback state and the idle counters are then committed together, and every participating client is supplied with the latest global model, accompanied by the fairness baseline and the deviation control signal (Lines 10-11).


The proposed approach evaluates the effectiveness of each client using a composite fitness score that integrates three metrics reflecting utility, fairness, and uncertainty penalty under non-IID data conditions. The utility and fairness are adjusted based on global convergence efficiency to ensure the balance between exploitation and exploration in the system.

\textbf{Operating assumptions and scope.} FedFACS assumes cooperative clients that report the scores computed by Algorithm 1; raw data stay local, and no further privacy or adversarial-robustness guarantee is claimed. Evaluation is in a synchronous simulation.

\subsection{FedFACS Methodology}
% REVISED (Replacement 5): KL divergence defined; per-example, batch and client scores (tags keep the original numbering)
Each candidate client is denoted by $i \in \mathcal{C}$. At round $t$, the central server sends $w_{\text{global}}^t$ to all candidate clients. The Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence of a distribution $P$ from a reference distribution $Q$ is $\mathrm{KL}(P \parallel Q)=\sum_{c}P(c)\log\frac{P(c)}{Q(c)}$; it is nonnegative, zero only when $P=Q$, and asymmetric (Kullback and Leibler 1951). For a classification task with label set $\mathcal{Y}$, client $i$ compares the class probabilities $P_i^t(\cdot\mid x)$ of its model after local training with the probabilities $Q^t(\cdot\mid x)$ of the unchanged global model received at round $t$, on the same client-local scoring examples (Amini et al. 2025). The per-example divergence is
\begin{equation}
d_i^t(x)=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{Y}}P_i^t(c\mid x)\log\frac{P_i^t(c\mid x)}{Q^t(c\mid x)}
\tag{5a}
\end{equation}
The scoring examples are divided into $B\ge 2$ nonempty batches $X_{i,b}^t$. The batch score and the client score, which weights every example equally, are
\begin{equation}
KL_{i,b}^t=\frac{1}{|X_{i,b}^t|}\sum_{x\in X_{i,b}^t}d_i^t(x)
\tag{5b}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\Delta KL_i^t=\frac{\sum_{b=1}^{B}|X_{i,b}^t|\,KL_{i,b}^t}{\sum_{b=1}^{B}|X_{i,b}^t|}
\label{eq:kl}
\end{equation}
The client-level KL score $\Delta KL_i^t$ (the symbol $\Delta$ denotes the divergence between local and global predictions) measures how far the predictive distribution of the locally trained model has moved from that of the global model on the client's scoring examples. A large value indicates that local training has altered the client's predictions substantially, so its update carries information not yet absorbed by the global model, and the client receives a higher utility term in the priority of Eq.~(9). A small value indicates that the local model reproduces the global predictive distribution, so that its update is expected to change the aggregate little and the utility term is low. The score requires no labels.

In FedFACS the utility term is combined with the waiting-time term $F_i^t\ge 1$ of Eq.~(6), which is larger in magnitude and is weighted by $1-a_i^t$, so that the priority is determined mainly by how long clients have waited and the KL score orders the clients with similar waiting times. Among such clients, those whose updates are more informative are therefore preferred, while no client is excluded by a low score. The experiments of Section~\ref{sec:score_analysis} support this use: under label skew the score is positively associated with the reduction in global validation loss obtained by aggregating the update, and more closely so than the magnitude of the parameter change when clients hold few classes. Under label noise a loss-based score would place clients with corrupted labels first among clients with similar waiting times, whereas the KL score does not. The score thus orders clients by a label-free indicator of update benefit that remains reliable under noise, and the waiting-time term keeps every client participating.

Fairness serves a vital function in preserving global model generalization. Without a fairness component, client selection approaches tend to repeatedly select a limited group of highly informative clients, leading to a biased global model toward particular data distributions while neglecting other less informative clients. The normalized fairness term in the proposed model is defined based on the consecutive idle rounds $r_i^t$ for client $i$, which is not selected up to round $t$. This is inspired by the Age Of Information (AOI) metric (Xu et al., 2024), and the average idle rounds across all participating clients at communication round $t$, represented by $\overline{r^t}$, which the server computes, as shown in Eq.~(6). The normalization is scale-invariant and prevents division by zero during the initial training round or when all clients are selected by the server, while adding 1 to the fraction as a baseline offset to ensure numerical stability. In addition, it evaluates a client’s need for participation relative to the current client activity.
\begin{equation}
F_i^t = 1 + \frac{r_i^t}{1 + \overline{r^t}}
\label{eq:fairness}
\end{equation}

% REVISED (Replacement 4): idle counter increments for unselected clients; bound derived; waiting-time guarantee by the guard of Sec. 4.3
The idle counter $r_i^t$ is the number of consecutive committed rounds before round $t$ in which client $i$ was not selected. It is held by the server and initialised as $r_i^1=0$ for all $i\in\mathcal{C}$. After each committed round it is updated by Eq.~(7), where $C_k^t$ denotes the cohort selected at round $t$; rounds whose staged state is discarded leave the counters unchanged.

\begin{equation}
r_i^{t+1} =
\begin{cases}
0 & \text{if } i \in C_k^t  \\
r_i^t + 1 & \text{if } i \notin C_k^t
\end{cases}
\label{eq:idle_update}
\end{equation}
Selection resets a counter to zero and every non-selected client increases its counter by one, so $r_i^t\ge 0$ grows linearly while a client waits. The fairness term of Eq.~(6), $F_i^t = 1 + r_i^t/(1+\overline{r^t})$ with the mean idle count $\overline{r^t}$ of Eq.~(19), is therefore bounded: since $r_i^t\le N\,\overline{r^t}$, it satisfies $1 \le F_i^t < N+1$. The term raises the priority of long-waiting clients; a maximum waiting time is guaranteed by the waiting-time guard of Section~\ref{sec:properties} (Proposition~2), and participation fairness is measured through the JFI of Eq.~(24).

% REVISED (Replacement 6): well-posed bounded feedback rule; priority renamed Pi; caveats stated
The proposed control mechanism is a bounded feedback rule that adapts the balance between informativeness and fairness to the observed training progress. It is well posed (Proposition~1 of Section~\ref{sec:properties}): the denominator below is positive and the mixing coefficient stays in $[0,1]$; participation fairness is additionally guaranteed by the waiting-time guard of the same section. The mixing coefficient $a_i^t \in [0,1]$, with $a_i^0=0.5$, is defined as follows:
% AUTHORS: confirm beta=0.9, lambda=1 and a^0=0.5 are the values to be reported (the original text never stated lambda)
\begin{equation}
a_i^t = \mathrm{clip}\left(
\beta \cdot a_i^{t-1} + (1-\beta)
\frac{
\nu^t \cdot (\Delta KL_i^t - F_i^t)
}{
\lambda \cdot F_i^t + \left|\nu^t \cdot (\Delta KL_i^t - F_i^t)\right|
},\ 0,\ 1\right)
\end{equation}

Here $\mathrm{clip}(x,0,1)=\min\{1,\max\{0,x\}\}$, $\beta=0.9$ is an exponential moving average smoothing parameter and $\lambda=1$ is a fairness regularization coefficient. Since $F_i^t \ge 1$ and $\lambda>0$, the denominator is positive. The term $\Delta KL_i^t - F_i^t$ provides a directional signal for decision-making, and the update smooths toward a signed feedback target in $[-1,1]$ before clipping to $[0,1]$. Because $F_i^t$ usually exceeds $\Delta KL_i^t$, a positive $\nu^t$ tends to lower $a_i^t$ and a negative $\nu^t$ tends to raise it.

The fairness term grows while a client remains unselected, so that long-waiting clients rise in the priority of Eq. (9), where $\Pi_i^t$ is the priority score of client $i$ at round $t$ and $a_i^t$ weights utility against fairness. Since $F_i^t \ge 1$ and the feedback rule keeps the mixing coefficient small, the priority is determined mainly by the fairness term: the utility term orders the clients with similar waiting times, so that no client is excluded by a low utility. Section~\ref{sec:score_analysis} shows that this is the regime in which FedFACS reaches its highest accuracy at near-uniform participation: weighting the two terms comparably, either with a constant coefficient of 0.5 or by rank normalisation, lowers the Jain index and does not raise accuracy, and no constant coefficient tested is significantly more accurate than the feedback rule, which sets the coefficient without tuning.
\begin{equation}
\Pi_i^t = a_i^t \cdot \Delta KL_i^t + (1 - a_i^t)\, F_i^t
\label{eq:priority}
\end{equation}

By adjusting the control parameter $a_i^t \in [0,1]$, the control mechanism dynamically balances informativeness against fairness. This parameter is updated based on the feedback deviation control signal $\nu^t$, which reflects the current convergence behavior of the global model.

% REVISED (Replacement 3): causal reference (no baseline run, no gamma); signal direction follows Eq. (8)
The deviation control signal $\nu^t$ is computed on the server from completed rounds only. It is the difference between a reference convergence rate $\Omega^t$ and the relative validation-loss reduction $\rho^{t-1}$ of the previous round (Eq.~(15)), as shown in Eq.~(10), and it is the feedback input of Eq.~(8). A positive $\nu^t$ means that the previous round converged more slowly than the reference; a non-positive value means that it converged at least as fast.
\begin{equation}
\nu^t = \Omega^t - \rho^{t-1}\qquad (t \ge 2)
\label{eq:deviation}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\Omega^t = \beta_{\text{ref}}\,\Omega^{t-1} + (1-\beta_{\text{ref}})\,\rho^{t-2}\qquad (t \ge 3)
\label{eq:reference_rate}
\end{equation}

Equation (11) defines the reference rate $\Omega^t$ as an exponential moving average, with smoothing constant $\beta_{\text{ref}}\in[0,1)$ ($\beta_{\text{ref}}=0.9$), of the loss reductions of earlier completed rounds of the same run. It is initialised with $\rho^{0}=0$, $\nu^{1}=0$ and $\Omega^{2}=0$, since the first round has no predecessor. The reference therefore requires no run of another algorithm, no acceleration index, no test data and no calibration traffic: it uses only the validation losses that the server already computes for Eq.~(15). Its effect on the mixing coefficient follows from Eq.~(8): since $\Delta KL_i^t - F_i^t$ is usually negative ($F_i^t\ge 1$), $\nu^t>0$ tends to lower $a_i^t$ and shifts the priority toward fairness, whereas $\nu^t\le 0$ tends to raise $a_i^t$ and shifts it toward utility. The signal thus measures how the latest round compares with the run's own recent progress, not with an external benchmark.

% REVISED (Replacement 5/6): batch variance defined with its mean (12a); fitness uses the renamed priority
Client-side uncertainty is introduced into the fitness score as a variance based penalty term. It quantifies the dispersion of the KL score across several small batches of the local client's dataset. It measures differences between scoring batches; it does not by itself separate noisy data from legitimate heterogeneity or rare classes. The uncertainty penalty term for client \textit{i} is defined from the batch mean (12a) and the sample variance (12):

\begin{equation}
\overline{KL}_i^t = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} KL_{i,b}^t
\tag{12a}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\sigma_i^2 = \frac{1}{B-1} \sum_{b=1}^{B} \left( KL_{i,b}^t - \overline{KL}_i^t \right)^2
\label{eq:variance}
\end{equation}

Where $B\ge 2$ indicates the total count of scoring batches (the sample variance is undefined for fewer batches), $\overline{KL}_i^t$ represents the average KL score over batches for client $i$, and $KL_{i,b}^t$ is the KL score of batch $b$ (Eq. (5b)).

The stability score is derived from Eqs. (5) and (12). It expresses the utility of a client's update net of its uncertainty and can be used to weight the local model updates in the aggregation step (Eq. (18)); the weighting that is used is stated below and compared in Section 5.4. The formula of the stability score is defined as follows:

\begin{equation}
s_i^t = \Delta KL_i^t - \mu \sigma_i^2
\label{eq:stability}
\end{equation}
The raw score may be negative; it is converted to nonnegative aggregation weights in Eq. (18). The following equation summarizes the calculation formula of the composite fitness score:
\begin{equation}
Fitness_i^t = \Pi_i^t - \mu \sigma_i^2
\label{eq:fitness}
\end{equation}

Where $\mu \ge 0$ is a regularization coefficient. Finally, each client sends its fitness score ${Fitness}_i^t$ to the server for evaluation; the server holds the number of consecutive idle rounds $r_i^t$. When the client is selected $i\in C_k^t$, it uploads its local model updates $w_i^t$, its stability score $s_i^t$ and its sample count $n_i$ for aggregation, and the server resets its idle counter at commit.

On the server-side, it receives fitness scores from all clients for evaluation. The server ranks the clients according to their fitness scores and adaptively determines the cohort size $m_t=|C_k^t|$ according to the convergence efficiency observed in the previous communication round, namely the relative reduction $\rho^{t-1}$ of the validation loss as shown in Eq. (15). Two predefined integer bounds, $C_{k_{\min}}$ and $C_{k_{\max}}$ (fixed in advance for each configuration), regulate the allowable cohort size range (McMahan et al. 2017; Li et al. 2020), thereby enabling an adaptive number of clients to be selected for aggregation while maintaining communication efficiency and mitigating network congestion, as defined in Eq. (16). Here $L_{global}^{t}$ is the validation loss after round $t$ and $L_{global}^{0}$ that of the initial model. Hence $\rho^{t}\le 1$: it is positive when the loss falls and negative when it rises, and a negative value is kept because it selects the maximum cohort. If the previous loss is zero or non-finite, $\rho^{t}$ is undefined and the round is cancelled with all state unchanged.
% REVISED (Replacement 3): Eq. (15) written for the completed round t (selection in round t uses rho^{t-1})
\begin{equation}
\rho^{t} = \frac{L_{{global}}^{t-1} - L_{{global}}^{t}}{L_{{global}}^{t-1}},\qquad L_{{global}}^{t-1}>0
\label{eq:convergence}
\end{equation}

% REVISED (Replacement 7): integer cohort size within its bounds
\begin{equation}
m_t = \min\left\{C_{k_{\max}},\ \max\left\{C_{k_{\min}},\ \left\lfloor C_{k_{\min}} + (C_{k_{\max}} - C_{k_{\min}})\big[1-\mathrm{clip}(\rho^{t-1},0,1)\big] + \tfrac{1}{2}\right\rfloor\right\}\right\}
\label{eq:ck_selection}
\end{equation}

A large $\rho^{t-1}$ means fast progress, so a smaller cohort suffices and the uploads fall; stagnation or a loss increase ($\rho^{t-1}\le 0$) and the first round select $C_{k_{\max}}$, since averaging more clients reduces the variance of the aggregate. Values in between are interpolated linearly and rounded to the nearest integer. Since $\rho^{t-1}$ alone keeps the cohort near $C_{k_{\max}}$, the ratio $\rho^{t-1}/\Omega^{t}$ can replace it, which spans the full range and lowers the uploads without a detectable change in accuracy (Table~\ref{tab:cohort}). Clients with a non-finite update are excluded; if fewer than $m_t$ remain, all are selected.

% REVISED (Replacement 8/9): nonnegative normalised weights; staged commit; Eq. (19) with N
Once the selected clients submit their local updates, the stability scores are converted into aggregation weights. For the selected clients let $s_{i,+}^t=\max(s_i^t,0)$ be the positive part of the raw score and $H^t=\sum_{j\in C_k^t}s_{j,+}^t$ its cohort sum. Following this, the server carries out the aggregation, which is formulated as follows (Eq. (17) restates Eq. (4)):

\begin{equation}
w_{{global}}^{t+1} = \sum_{i \in C_k^t} S_i^t \, w_i^t
\label{eq:stability_weighted_update}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
S_i^t = (1-\kappa)\,\dfrac{n_i}{\sum_{j\in C_k^t} n_j} + \kappa\,q_i^t,\qquad q_i^t=\begin{cases}
\dfrac{s_{i,+}^t}{H^t}, & H^t>0,\\[1.5ex]
\dfrac{n_i}{\sum_{j\in C_k^t} n_j}, & H^t=0
\end{cases}
\label{eq:stability_norm}
\end{equation}

The coefficient $\kappa\in[0,1]$ sets the weighting: $\kappa=0$ gives sample-count weights and $\kappa=1$ gives the stability weights $q_i^t$. FedFACS uses $\kappa=0$ in all reported experiments; Section 5.4 compares the two choices. For numerical stability the first branch of $q_i^t$ is evaluated as $z_i^t/\sum_{j\in C_k^t}z_j^t$ with $z_i^t=s_{i,+}^t/\hat{s}^{\,t}$ and $\hat{s}^{\,t}=\max_{j\in C_k^t}s_{j,+}^t$. All choices of $\kappa$ give $S_i^t\ge 0$ and $\sum_{i\in C_k^t}S_i^t=1$, and they are defined for negative scores and for identical client models (all scores zero). Frozen tensors and nontrainable buffers keep their round-start values. A selected client with $S_i^t=0$ incurs upload cost and resets its idle counter but does not contribute to that round's aggregate.

After aggregation, the server evaluates the candidate model on a separate validation set and stages $\rho^t$, $\Omega^{t+1}$, $\nu^{t+1}$, $r_i^{t+1}$ and $\overline{r^{t+1}}$ (Eq.~(19)). The model, the feedback state and the counters are committed together only if all numerical and protocol checks pass; otherwise the staged state is discarded and the consumed resources are still recorded. Messages carry run and round identifiers, and duplicate or stale messages are rejected. The committed global model parameters $w_{{global}}^{t+1}$, $\overline{r^{t+1}}$, and $\nu^{t+1}$ are then broadcast to all $N$ candidate clients for the following training round.

\begin{equation}
\overline{r^{t+1}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} r_i^{t+1}
\label{eq:avg_idle_correct}
\end{equation}

The proposed approach introduces a fitness scoring method that ensures the selection of high-quality and and informative clients, while preserving fair participation according to client’s informativeness and the current level of model convergence. This allows the framework to explore clients that may provide useful information during stable improvement, allowing them to contribute. However, it forces the system to exploit the more informative clients during degradation, preventing bias in the global model and preserving balance when system stagnates. As a result, the proposed approach enhances the overall performance by improving accuracy, reducing communication overhead by selecting the more contributing clients to send their updates, and ensuring fair participation. 

Furthermore, the aggregation weights of Eq.~(18) are nonnegative and sum to one for every choice of $\kappa$. With $\kappa=0$ each selected client contributes in proportion to its sample count, so that the aggregate is consistent with the objective of Eq.~(1); with $\kappa>0$ the weights shift towards clients with a high stability score. Selection is governed by the fitness of Eq.~(14) and the waiting-time guard, and aggregation by the weights of Eq.~(18); Section~\ref{sec:score_analysis} reports participation counts, waiting times and the cumulative aggregation influence of the clients.

\subsection{Properties of FedFACS}\label{sec:properties}
This subsection states what the design guarantees. Throughout, $m_t=|C_k^t|$ is the cohort size of Eq.~(16) with $C_{k_{\min}}\le m_t\le C_{k_{\max}}$, $\mathcal{C}$ is the set of $N$ clients and $r_i^t$ is the idle counter of Eq.~(7).

\textbf{Waiting-time guard.} To make participation fairness a guarantee rather than only a tendency of the fairness term, selection uses an age guard with threshold $R=\lceil N/C_{k_{\min}}\rceil$. Let $U^t=\{i\in\mathcal{C}: r_i^t\ge R\}$ be the overdue clients, ordered by decreasing $r_i^t$ and, for equal counters, by ascending identifier. If $|U^t|\ge m_t$, the cohort consists of the first $m_t$ clients of $U^t$; otherwise it consists of $U^t$ and of the $m_t-|U^t|$ clients of $\mathcal{C}\setminus U^t$ with the highest fitness (Eq.~(14)). When no client is overdue the rule is exactly Eq.~(3).

\textbf{Proposition 1 (well-posedness).} In every round: (i) the denominator of Eq.~(8) is at least $\lambda>0$ and $a_i^t\in[0,1]$; (ii) $1\le F_i^t<N+1$; (iii) $m_t$ is an integer with $C_{k_{\min}}\le m_t\le C_{k_{\max}}$; (iv) the weights satisfy $S_i^t\ge 0$ and $\sum_{i\in C_k^t}S_i^t=1$.

\textit{Proof.} (i) $F_i^t\ge 1$ gives $\lambda F_i^t\ge\lambda$, and the clip in Eq.~(8) bounds $a_i^t$. (ii) $r_i^t\le\sum_j r_j^t=N\,\overline{r^t}$, hence $F_i^t=1+r_i^t/(1+\overline{r^t})<1+N$. (iii) and (iv) follow from the clamping and rounding of Eq.~(16) and from the two branches of Eq.~(18). \hfill$\square$

\textbf{Proposition 2 (bounded waiting).} Assume $m_t\ge C_{k_{\min}}$ in every committed round. With the guard, every client is selected within $W=R+\lceil N/C_{k_{\min}}\rceil$ consecutive committed rounds, i.e. $r_i^t\le W-1$ for all $i$ and $t$.

\textit{Proof.} Let client $i$ first reach $r_i^{t_0}=R$; from $t_0$ on it is overdue until it is selected. Let $A^t$ be the number of overdue clients ranked ahead of $i$, so $A^{t_0}\le N-1$. A client that becomes overdue after $t_0$ has a smaller counter than $i$ and ranks behind it, and clients ahead of $i$ that are not selected keep their order because all counters grow by one. In a round in which $i$ is not selected, all $m_t\ge C_{k_{\min}}$ selected clients are ahead of $i$, so $A^t$ falls by at least $C_{k_{\min}}$; once $A^t+1\le m_t$ the client is selected. Hence $i$ is selected at a round $t_0+k$ with $k\le\lceil N/C_{k_{\min}}\rceil-1$, when its counter is $R+k\le W-1$. \hfill$\square$

For $N=100$ and $C_{k_{\min}}=5$ the threshold is $R=20$ and no client waits more than 39 rounds, whatever the scores are. The longest waiting times observed were 6, 17, 22 and 43 rounds for $N=50,100,200,400$ (thresholds $R=10,20,40,80$), so the guard was never active in the 84 FedFACS runs (nor in the 45 runs of its A2 variant) of Section 5, and the reported results are those of the unguarded rule. The guarantee concerns participation, not influence: a selected client with $S_i^t=0$ does not change the aggregate.

\textbf{Proposition 3 (bounded round-to-round movement).} Let local training clip the gradient norm at $G$, use learning rate $\eta$ and at most $K$ local steps per round. Then $\|w_i^t-w_{\text{global}}^t\|\le\eta KG$ for every client, and, for any selection and any weights with $S_i^t\ge 0$ and $\sum_i S_i^t=1$, $\|w_{\text{global}}^{t+1}-w_{\text{global}}^t\|\le\eta KG$.

\textit{Proof.} Each clipped step moves the parameters by at most $\eta G$, so $K$ steps move them by at most $\eta KG$; the aggregate is a convex combination of the local models, and the norm is convex. \hfill$\square$

The aggregate therefore cannot move by more than $\eta KG$ in one round, whichever clients are selected or how they are weighted; clients whose update is not finite are excluded, and a round is committed only if all checks pass (Section 4.1).

\section{Experimental Evaluation}\label{sec5}

\subsection{Datasets}

For performance evaluation of the proposed framework, extensive experimental evaluations were carried out using four widely used benchmark multimedia datasets. These datasets include MNIST, FMNIST, CIFAR-10, and the COVID-19 Radiography dataset. MNIST (LeCun et al. 2010) and FMNIST (Xiao et al. 2017) contain 60,000 training images and 10,000 test images, with each sample represented as a 28 \ensuremath{\times} 28 grayscale image. MNIST covers handwritten digit recognition, while FMNIST consists of images from ten clothing-related categories. In addition, CIFAR-10 extends the evaluation to natural RGB images, comprising 60,000 samples of size 32 \ensuremath{\times} 32 pixels organized into ten categories (Krizhevsky and Hinton 2009). To extend the evaluation, the COVID-19 radiography database was used to assess the framework under a real-world medical imaging scenario. It comprises chest radiographs from three classes: COVID-19, normal, and viral pneumonia (Rahman et al. 2020).  

To assess the proposed framework under heterogeneous data conditions, the training data are partitioned using four distinct scenarios to model varying degrees data heterogeneity across clients. In the first scenario (S1), a Dirichlet distribution $\mathrm{\textit{Dir}}(\alpha)$ is applied to distribute training samples among clients with the distribution parameter $\alpha \in \{0.1, 0.5\}$ to generate imbalanced datasets in both size and label distribution, where a smaller value of $\alpha$ leads to higher data skewness (Iyer. 2023). In the second scenario (S2), clients are randomly assigned data from two classes (Zhai et al. 2024). In the third scenario (S3), a number of noisy data is generated and distributed across the clients with a noise level ranging from 0\% to 45\% per client under IID conditions. In the fourth scenario (S4), three clients contain data from all ten classes with noise levels of 10\% and 20\%, while the remaining clients contain data from only nine classes with the noise patterns described in S3, under non-IID conditions (Shi et al. 2023).

\subsection{Model and experimental setup}

\textbf{Benchmark.} The comparisons are made across established federated learning baselines and recent adaptive selection methods. The proposed FedFACS framework is compared against several approaches, such as FedAvg (McMahan et al. 2017), FedProx (Li et al. 2020), DivFL (Balakrishnan et al. 2022), Power-of-Choice (Cho et al. 2022), and the approach introduced by Zhai et al. (2024) to evaluate performance and communication efficiency. 

Furthermore, FedFACS is compared against a Random baseline that selects clients randomly in each communication round, Power-of-Choice, and AHIBCS (Liu et al. 2025), a recent adaptive selection method. This comparison specifically evaluates the accuracy and convergence speed of FedFACS against the most closely related existing selection strategy, thereby validating the robustness of the presented framework.

In addition, for evaluating the balance between fairness and performance, the proposed approach is compared against four client selection strategies: a Random baseline, a Greedy strategy that prioritizes clients with the highest reputation, the RBFF method proposed by Song et al. (2022), and the FairFedCS approach introduced by Shi et al. (2023).

\textbf{Model}. The proposed FedFACS approach was implemented in Python using PyTorch and Torch-vision, employing a fine-tuned ResNet-18 (He et al. 2016) to achieve stable training. This architecture delivers high accuracy with low computational complexity, where the early blocks including the convolutional layer 1 and layers 1-3 are frozen, while the layer 4 and the final fully connected layer are trained and adjusted for 10 classes.

\textbf{Hyperparameters}. The experimental evaluation is conducted under three distinct configurations, each is designed to test the proposed model under different conditions. For configuration A, all the methods follow the same hyperparameter settings as in (Zhai et al. 2024). The federated learning environment is simulated with a single centralized server and 100 decentralized clients. The experiments are carried out for 200 training rounds, under a local training setup consisting of 10 epochs, a batch size of 32, $\beta = 0.9$, and the maximum client sampling rate $C_{k\max}$= 0.1. The models were trained via Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD), with learning rates of 0.05 and 0.01 are adopted for CIFAR-10 and both FMNIST and MNIST, respectively. The proximal coefficient in FedProx is set to 0.1. For the Power-of-Choice (Pow-D), 10 highest-loss clients are selected out of a randomly sampled set of 20. These experiments are to compare the proposed model against FedAvg, FedProx, Power-of-Choice, and the approach of (Zhai et al. 2024), highlighting the performance and communication efficiency under data distributions described in scenarios 1 and 2.

Configuration B follows the experimental setup proposed in (Liu et al. 2025). Additional experiments were carried out using the FMNIST, CIFAR-10, and COVID-19 benchmark datasets. Model training was performed over 100 communication rounds on FMNIST and 350 rounds on both CIFAR-10 and COVID-19, with the learning rates of 0.001, 0.1, and 0.01, respectively. The FMNIST and CIFAR-10 experiments were performed with 100 participating clients, whereas the COVID-19 experiments were conducted using 30 participating clients. For the competing methods Random, Pow-D, and AHIBCS, at every training iteration, a total of 15 clients were chosen at random, from which 10 clients were selected for model aggregation.

For configuration C, additional experiments are conducted with learning rates configured to 0.005 for CIFAR-10 and 0.01 for FMNIST, respectively. Training continues until no further improvement in the global loss is observed over 20 consecutive communication rounds. A batch size of 64 is adopted with 20 local training epochs. In FairFedCS, the control parameter $\sigma$ is fixed at 0.6. This experiments evaluates the proposed model against Random, Greedy, RBFF, and FairFedCS, focusing on fairness-accuracy balance under data distributions described in scenarios 3 and 4.

\textbf{Performance metrics}. The proposed framework is examined with respect to performance, fairness, and communication efficiency using the following evaluation metrics:

\textit{Accuracy}: refers to the rate of correct classifications produced by the trained model over the test samples.

\textit{Round-to-accuracy}: defines the first completed training round at which the accuracy reaches a specified target on the validation data; a run that does not reach the target within its budget is recorded as not reached. This metric provides an assessment of the convergence efficiency of the client selection strategy, where fewer communication rounds indicate faster convergence.

\textit{Recall and F1 score}: Recall reflects the true positive rate, while the F1 score provides a comprehensive and balanced assessment of classification capability by incorporating precision and recall (Yacouby and Axman 2020; Liu et al. 2025). 
\begin{equation}
\text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}
\label{eq:recall}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}
\label{eq:precision}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
F1 = 2 \times \frac{\text{Precision} \times \text{Recall}}
{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}}
\label{eq:f1_score}
\end{equation}



In the formulation, $TP$, $FP$, and $FN$ indicate correctly detected, incorrectly predicted, and missed positive instances, respectively. These metrics evaluate the performance, stability, and generalization ability of FedFACS under non-IID conditions.


\textit{Cumulative number of selected clients until convergence  ($C_{total}$)}: indicates the overall number of client updates uploaded for aggregation across the first $T$ training rounds:

\begin{equation}
C_{\text{total}} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \left|C_k^t\right|
\label{eq:communication}
\end{equation}

% REVISED (Replacement 10): communication accounting (new equation, tag keeps the original numbering)
The total communication $\mathrm{Comm}_{\mathrm{total}}(T)$, in bytes over the first $T$ rounds, also includes downloads, auxiliary traffic and any setup or calibration traffic:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Comm}_{\mathrm{total}}(T)=\mathrm{Comm}_{\mathrm{setup}}+\mathrm{Comm}_{\mathrm{cal}}+\mathrm{Comm}_{\mathrm{final}}+\sum_{t\in\mathcal{R}(T)}\left(\mathrm{Comm}^t_{\mathrm{down}}+\mathrm{Comm}^t_{\mathrm{up}}+\mathrm{Comm}^t_{\mathrm{aux}}\right)
\tag{23a}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{Comm}^t_{\mathrm{down}}$, $\mathrm{Comm}^t_{\mathrm{up}}$ and $\mathrm{Comm}^t_{\mathrm{aux}}$ are the model-download, update-upload and auxiliary traffic of round $t$ (in bytes), $\mathrm{Comm}_{\mathrm{setup}}$, $\mathrm{Comm}_{\mathrm{cal}}$ and $\mathrm{Comm}_{\mathrm{final}}$ are the setup, calibration (zero for FedFACS) and final-model traffic, and $\mathcal{R}(T)$ is the set of attempted rounds, including partial and cancelled ones.

\textit{Jain's Fairness Index (JFI)}: quantifies the fairness of participation on the quality-adjusted participation $p_i/q_i$ (Shi et al. 2024; Cho et al. 2020).
\begin{equation}
JFI = \frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{p_i}{q_i}\right)^2}
{N \cdot \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left(\frac{p_i}{q_i}\right)^2}
\label{eq:jfi}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
q_i = (\text{number of labels}) \times (1 - \text{percentage of noisy data})
\label{eq:qi}
\end{equation}

For client $i$, $p_i$ reflects its rate of participation in model aggregation, while $N$ denotes the overall client population, and $q_i$ represents the quality of its locally stored dataset (Shi et al. 2023). Therefore $JFI = 1$ means participation proportional to quality, which equals equal participation only when all $q_i$ are equal. The minimum value is $1/N$, and a value between $0.5$ and $1$ does not imply that every client participates (serving 90 of 100 equal-quality clients uniformly gives $0.9$). Because $q_i$ depends on the noise level, JFI differences may partly reflect selection by data quality.

\subsection{Experimental Results and Analysis}

The evaluation of FedFACS was conducted across a range of heterogeneous data distribution conditions, with the experiments performed on a system using an Nvidia RTX 3090 GPU and 32 GB of RAM.

The relative contribution of each component in FedFACS is individually evaluated through a comprehensive ablation study. The components are the full FedFACS, FedFACS-A1 which removes both of adaptive aggregation and cohort sizing (Ablation 1), where client selection is performed with a constant sampling size of ($C_k^t=10$) and static weighted aggregation is used (McMahan et al. 2017), and FedFACS-A2 that utilizing dynamic cohort sizing with ($C_{k_{\min}} = 8$) and ($C_{k_{\max}} = 10$) removes adaptive aggregation only (Ablation 2). The experimental setup follows configuration A, and The target accuracy is determined as the maximum accuracy obtained by FedAvg under the corresponding experiments with data partitioning conditions presented in scenarios 1 and 2.

Tables 1 and 2 demonstrate the improvements achieved by FedFACS-A1 that incorporates the proposed adaptive client selection mechanism, including higher predictive accuracy while reducing the communication rounds required to attain the target accuracy. Moreover, dynamic cohort sizing (FedFACS-A2) changes the accuracy only slightly. Relative to a fixed cohort of $C_{k_{\max}}$ clients, it saves the fraction by which the mean cohort size falls below $C_{k_{\max}}$; this fraction cannot exceed $1-C_{k_{\min}}/C_{k_{\max}}$, so it grows with the distance between the two bounds (Table~\ref{tab:cohort}). Furthermore, the full FedFACS model replaces the sample-count weights of FedFACS-A2 by the stability weights of Eq.~(18) with $\kappa=1$. The comparison of the two weightings is repeated in Section~\ref{sec:score_analysis} on nine dataset--partition settings, where the sample-count weighting gives the more stable final model.

As illustrated in Figs. 8 and 9, FedFACS outperforms the baseline approaches across all experimental datasets. On FMNIST, FedFACS achieves an accuracy of 87.1\% improving Random, FedProx, and AHIBCS by 6.74\%, 4.31\%, and 1.40\%, respectively. Similarly, on CIFAR-10, FedFACS achieves an accuracy of 54.3\%, corresponding to improvements of 5.85\%, 10.82\%, and 2.26\% over the baseline methods. For the COVID-19 dataset, FedFACS demonstrates its effectiveness by achieving the highest classification accuracy of 95.3\%, outperforming AHIBCS by 0.5\%. These findings demonstrate that FedFACS successfully selects informative clients while maintaining robust performance under diverse datasets and application domains.   

Table 4 compares the recall and F1-score obtained by the evaluated methods (Liu et al. 2025), showing that FedFACS records the highest F1-score on all considered datasets and demonstrates competitive recall performance, demonstrating more reliable discrimination between classes. Table 5 evaluates communication efficiency by reporting the communication rounds together with the total number of aggregated client participations needed to attain predefined accuracy levels of 83\%, 50\%, and 91\% on FMNIST, CIFAR-10, and COVID-19, respectively. Although AHIBCS requires fewer communication rounds on FMNIST and CIFAR-10, FedFACS achieves better classification performance with a slight increase in number of communication rounds. Specifically, FedFACS requires 35 and 151 rounds on FMNIST and CIFAR-10, respectively, compared with 27 and 144 rounds for AHIBCS. However, adaptive client selection enables FedFACS to reduce communication overhead, resulting in 266 cumulative aggregated client participation on FMNIST and 1381 on CIFAR-10, representing reductions of 1.48\% and 4.10\%, respectively, compared with AHIBCS. On the COVID-19 dataset, FedFACS achieves the target accuracy in 103 rounds, compared with 110 rounds for AHIBCS, representing a 6.36\% reduction in communication rounds with fewer participating clients.

FedFACS achieves slightly higher \textit{JFI} values than FairFedCS in most scenarios, with gains ranging from (+0.006) to (+0.026) as shown in Fig. 11. These results indicate that FedFACS can improve fairness under non-IID and noisy-data settings while avoiding fairness degradation. This results indicates fair client participation across training rounds where all clients are selected with varying frequencies according to their impact on the global model performance. Moreover, FedFACS achieves the stated performance with fewer participating clients by using dynamic cohort sizing based on the global model’s convergence state $(C_{k_{\min}} < C_k^t \le m)$ instead of selecting a predefined client sampling size \textit{m} in every communication round as in baseline methods $(m = C_k^t)$. 


Although improving fairness often leads to a degradation of accuracy, the proposed FedFACS effectively manages this trade-off by avoiding forced uniform participation (\textit{JFI}=1), which negatively affects overall model accuracy by limiting contribution from highly informative clients, especially under heterogeneous data distributions. Instead, FedFACS employs an adaptive control mechanism that regulates client selection frequency based on their real-time impact on model convergence. Furthermore, the sample-count aggregation keeps the global model stable at the end of training (Section~\ref{sec:score_analysis}). This design enables informative clients to participate more frequently while preventing client starvation. Consequently, FedFACS achieves higher fairness while preserving or improving accuracy, with fewer participating clients and reduced communication cost.


Finally, the full FedFACS model improves accuracy, communication efficiency and, fairness by integrating adaptive client selection, dynamic cohort sizing, and normalised aggregation. This integration leverages the proposed client selection mechanism to fairly identify and select highly effective clients and maximize the effectiveness of informative and stable clients while preserving fair participation for less informative clients. This synergy improves training efficiency by accelerating and stabilizing convergence, showing that each component is crucial for balancing high model accuracy with low communication costs while ensuring fair client participation.

The performance analysis demonstrate that the proposed framework delivers superior results compared with current benchmark methods across different heterogeneous data scenarios on CIFAR-10, MNIST, and FMNIST datasets. Consequently, FedFACS improves global performance, ensures fairness, and reduces the communication overhead by accelerating convergence by achieving target performance with fewer communication rounds and selecting fewer clients.

\subsection{Analysis of the selection score}\label{sec:score_analysis}
This subsection examines the components of the selection score of Eqs.~(5)--(14). It uses a ResNet-18 with frozen early layers, 100 clients, 100 rounds and cohorts of 5 to 10 clients, with 3 seeds for the ablations (5 for the utility analysis) and 95\% bootstrap intervals. The absolute accuracies therefore differ from those of Tables~1 and~2. All variants in Table~\ref{tab:mixing} use sample-count aggregation and differ from FedFACS only in the mixing coefficient $a$.

\textbf{Utility of the KL score.} For every candidate client, in rounds 1, 5, 10, 25, 50 and 100, we computed the marginal gain, i.e. the reduction in global validation loss obtained when that client's update alone is added to the global model with weight $1/m_t$, and its Spearman rank correlation $\rho$ with the client indicators of Table~\ref{tab:kl_validity}. The KL score is positively associated with the gain under every label-skew partition ($\rho=0.39$ to $0.73$). The global cross-entropy of the client's examples is the stronger predictor under label skew but reverses its sign under label noise ($\rho=-0.77$), because clients with corrupted labels have a high loss and degrade the aggregate; the KL score shows no such reversal ($\rho=0.12$, not significant). Selecting with a loss-based utility instead of KL changes accuracy by $+0.57$ [$-0.33$, $+1.64$] points on CIFAR-10 and $+0.25$ [$-0.08$, $+0.48$] on FMNIST, i.e. not detectably. The KL score is therefore a valid, label-free and noise-robust utility indicator. Compared with the raw parameter drift $\lVert w_i^t-w_{\mathrm{global}}^t\rVert$ (Table~\ref{tab:kl_drift}), it is the better predictor under the two-class partition on both datasets and is not distinguishable from it under Dirichlet $\alpha=0.1$.

\textbf{Fairness term and mixing coefficient.} At the diagnostic rounds 5 to 100 the fairness term has a median of 1.88 and the utility score $\Delta KL_i^t$ a median of 0.73, and the coefficients have a median of 0.10 (below). The weighted utility term $a_i^t\Delta KL_i^t$ is therefore a median 3\% of $(1-a_i^t)F_i^t$, the priority of Eq.~(9) is rank-correlated with $F_i^t$ (Spearman 0.92 against 0.27 for $\Delta KL_i^t$), and 98.9\% of the selected clients have a fairness term above the median: the priority is determined mainly by the waiting time, and the utility term orders the clients with similar waiting times. Table~\ref{tab:mixing} shows that this regime is the one that works. Without the fairness term ($a=1$) the Jain index falls to 0.56 and 0.35, 10.0\% and 20.3\% of the clients are never selected, and accuracy decreases by 2.6 and 2.2 points. A balanced constant $a=0.5$ lowers the Jain index to 0.92 and 0.94, lengthens the longest wait to 14--16 rounds, and lowers accuracy (best round, CIFAR-10: $-1.25$ [$-1.90$, $-0.73$]; last 10 rounds, FMNIST: $-1.53$ [$-3.42$, $-0.29$]). Rank-normalising both terms so that they carry equal weight, together with the waiting-time guard and with $a\in\{0.25,0.5\}$, gave the same outcome: the Jain index fell to 0.94--0.99, the longest wait rose to 17--20 rounds, and accuracy did not improve ($-0.5$ to $-0.8$ points). At the diagnostic rounds 5 to 100 the coefficients of the clients have a median of 0.10 (mean 0.14; 5th--95th percentile 0.001--0.33). In accuracy, no constant is significantly better than the feedback rule in any comparison, and the rule is significantly better in some ($a=0.05$ on CIFAR-10, best round: $-0.80$ [$-1.91$, $-0.10$] for the constant; $a=0.10$ on FMNIST, last 10 rounds: $-0.90$ [$-1.33$, $-0.30$]); the constants $a=0.05$ and $a=0.10$ differ from it by at most one round in the longest wait and by at most 0.002 in the Jain index. The rule thus provides this operating point without requiring the coefficient to be tuned.

\textbf{Aggregation weights.} Replacing the stability weights ($\kappa=1$ in Eq.~(18)) by sample-count weights ($\kappa=0$), with the selection unchanged, leaves participation unchanged (Jain index 0.995--0.999, longest wait 10--13 rounds, no unselected client in all nine settings) and removes most of the oscillation of the global model at the end of training: in the five of nine dataset--partition settings with a detectable difference the last-10-round accuracy increases (by up to 9.6 [7.4, 12.9] points on MNIST with $\alpha=0.5$), in none does it decrease, and the round-to-round fluctuation of the test accuracy falls from 5.0 to 1.5 points on CIFAR-10 and from 3.9 to 1.0 points on FMNIST ($\alpha=0.5$). The best-round accuracy is higher in four settings, not distinguishable in four, and lower in one (CIFAR-10, $\alpha=0.1$). FedFACS therefore uses $\kappa=0$.

\textbf{Cohort size.} Eq.~(16) with the absolute loss reduction $\rho^{t-1}$ keeps the cohort at $C_{k_{\max}}$ in 75\% of the rounds and never below 7 clients in the 45 runs of the reproduction study (mean 9.71 [9.68, 9.75] clients, i.e.\ 2.9\% fewer uploads than a fixed cohort of ten). Measuring the efficiency against the reference pace of the run (the ratio $\rho^{t-1}/\Omega^{t}$ in place of $\rho^{t-1}$ in Eq.~(16)) uses the whole range from 5 to 10 clients: in Table~\ref{tab:cohort} the mean cohort falls from 9.83 to 9.10 clients and the upload volume by 7.5\% relative to the absolute rule (9.0\% relative to a fixed cohort), with best-round accuracy $-0.29$ [$-0.57$, $+0.05$] and last-10-round accuracy $-0.08$ [$-0.55$, $+0.41$] points, Jain index 0.997 against 0.998 and no unselected client; the longest wait rises from 10.8 to 14.0 rounds, below the threshold $R=20$ of the waiting-time guard. \textbf{Participation and aggregation influence.} With the feedback rule and sample-count aggregation (45 runs, nine settings) every client was selected at least once, the Jain index of the selection counts was 0.995--0.999 and the longest wait 10--13 rounds, below the guard threshold; the weakest tenth of the clients reached 63.2\% and 76.3\% accuracy on CIFAR-10 and FMNIST (Table~\ref{tab:mixing}). The cumulative aggregation influence of a client, the sum of its weights $S_i^t$ over the rounds, was positive for every client; on average over the runs the least influential client had 0.35 and the most influential 2.24 times the mean influence (Gini coefficient 0.21). The smallest weight of any selected client in any round was $2\times10^{-3}$ with sample-count weights and $4\times10^{-5}$ with stability weights, so that no weight was zero or negative.

\textbf{Uncertainty penalty.} The batch variance does not separate noisy from clean clients (rank AUC 0.45 [0.39, 0.52]), so it acts as a stabilising term in the fitness of Eq.~(14) and not as a noise detector. With sample-count aggregation, removing it changes the best-round accuracy by $-0.04$ [$-0.22$, $+0.12$] points on CIFAR-10 and $-0.14$ [$-0.35$, $+0.27$] on FMNIST and lowers the last-10-round accuracy by 0.39 [0.14, 0.77] points on FMNIST, so the penalty improves the stability of the final model on FMNIST and is neutral on CIFAR-10. In the 96 main runs of FedFACS and FedFACS-A2 the mean mixing coefficient decreased in 98.2\% of the 4,316 rounds in which $\nu^t>0$, as Eq.~(8) predicts.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study presents FedFACS, an adaptive and fairness-aware client selection mechanism designed to improve communication efficiency while preserving model accuracy and generalization under heterogeneous data distributions. The proposed framework adapts the client selection strategy according to the convergence performance of the global model, maintaining a balance between informativeness and fairness. Moreover, the aggregation uses nonnegative normalised weights, with sample-count weighting as the default, which gives the more stable global model at the end of training in the comparison of Section~\ref{sec:score_analysis}. Evaluation results across diverse benchmark datasets under various data partitioning scenarios show that the proposed FedFACS provides higher classification accuracy and speeds up convergence, while selecting the minimum number of clients to reduce communication overhead while preserving fairness in client selection compared with the best-performing baseline methods.

While FedFACS handles data heterogeneity and label noise, its robustness against advanced adversarial attacks and Byzantine participants remains unexplored. Future work will extend the framework by integrating data poisoning attack detection and mitigation mechanisms while supporting complex multimodal datasets for real-world applications.
