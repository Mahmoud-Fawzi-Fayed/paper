30: 1. Hidden communication and computation cost. According to Algorithms 1–2, the server broadcasts the model to all clients, and all clients train for E epochs and compute KL and fitness. Only the upload is reduced. Baselines such as FedAvg train only the sampled clients, and Pow-D's probing cost is not counted. Downlink and local-compute costs must be reported; otherwise the “communication-efficient” claim is misleading and the comparison is unfair.

31: 2. Dependence on a baseline run (Eq. 11). The reference rate Ω^t = γ·ρ_base comes from a prior run of a state-of-the-art method, which is unavailable in practice. The controller is effectively tuned to the curve of the competitor it is later compared against. γ is undefined and neither tuned nor analyzed.

32: 3. Incorrect update rule (Eq. 7). An unselected client's idle counter stays at r_i^t and never increases. This contradicts the text, under which fairness grows as a client waits. The fairness mechanism as written cannot work.

33: 4. Ill-posed control law (Eq. 8). The denominator λF + ν(ΔKL − F) can reach zero or become negative. The claimed direction (ν > 0 ⇒ larger α) does not hold in general, since F ≥ 1 usually exceeds ΔKL. λ is never specified. Calling it “Lyapunov-inspired” is unjustified without a Lyapunov function or a stability proof; as written it is a smoothed heuristic.

34: 5. Scale mismatch in Eq. 9. The fairness term F ≥ 1 is unbounded, while ΔKL is typically ≪ 1. Fairness will dominate regardless of α, and the “+1 for numerical stability” does not justify this design.

35: 6. Questionable utility definition. Post-training KL between local and global predictions measures client drift, which under non-IID data is typically harmful (the motivation for FedProx/FedNova). No theoretical or empirical evidence shows that high KL means high utility. The sum over x ∈ X is also ill-defined.

36: 7. Aggregation weights (Eqs. 4, 13, 17–18). s = ΔKL − μσ² can be negative, yielding negative aggregation weights. Dataset size n_i is dropped, which is inconsistent with the objective in Eq. 1. Eq. 4 is unnormalized while Eq. 17 is normalized.

37: 8. Inconsistent time indexing and bounds. ν^{t+1} = Ω^{t+1} − ρ^{t+1} requires future losses. Eqs. 10 and 15–16 mix ρ^t and ρ^{t−1}. If ρ < 0, then (1 − ρ) > 1 and C_k^t exceeds C_kmax. Rounding of the cohort size and the treatment of rounds 1–2 are unspecified. Notation (N/K/C) is inconsistent.

38: 9. Negligible dynamic range of the cohort. The cohort varies only within 8–10 clients (C_kmin = 8, C_kmax = 10), so the claimed communication savings are inherently small. The reported “≈15% fewer clients per round” (Ablation 2) is impossible with these bounds (maximum 20%) and inconsistent with Fig. 4 and Table 3, where average cohort sizes are ≈9.6–9.97.

39: 10. Numerical inconsistencies in the experiments.

40: •       Table 5: FedFACS on COVID-19 uses 1047 clients over 103 rounds (≈10.17 per round), exceeding the stated cap of m = 10. On FMNIST, 266/35 ≈ 7.6 is below C_kmin = 8. The Configuration B bounds are not reported.

41: •       Table 4: on balanced test sets (CIFAR-10, FMNIST), macro-recall should equal accuracy. Recall for CIFAR-10 is ≈4 points below accuracy, and F1 exceeds recall by 4–10 points in all rows. For CIFAR-10 (accuracy 54.3%) and FMNIST, the reported F1 values appear arithmetically implausible for macro-averages. The averaging method must be stated and the raw confusion matrices provided.

42: •       The text reports FMNIST-S2 accuracy of 83.08%, but Table 1 gives 83.80. In that cell the ablated FedFACS-A1 (83.81) is better than the full model.

43: •       The text names “Random, FedProx and AHIBCS” in the Configuration B comparison, but the tables and figures use Pow-D rather than FedProx.

44: •       The FMNIST-S2 round reduction is stated as 2.2%, but (108 − 105)/108 ≈ 2.8%.

45: •       The CIFAR-10 accuracy for Random differs between Fig. 8 and Fig. 9 (51.1 vs. 51.3).

46: •       Accuracy of ≈47–54% on CIFAR-10 is unusually low for a fine-tuned ResNet-18, and the “fine-tuned for 10 classes” description conflicts with the three-class COVID-19 dataset.

47: 11. Misleading aggregate claims in the abstract. The 0.44% recall “improvement” averages +1.64, +1.15 and −1.48 (FedFACS is worse than AHIBCS on COVID-19 recall). Relative and absolute gains are mixed (e.g., “1.2% relative” is actually 1.2 absolute points; “0.5%” on COVID-19 is absolute). The “best performing existing method” differs by configuration (Zhai, AHIBCS, FedFairCS) and these are never compared in one common setting.

48: 12. No statistical rigor. Results appear to come from a single seed with no standard deviations or confidence intervals. The accuracy curves in Fig. 5 fluctuate by ±4–5 points, so differences of 1–3 rounds, 0.3–1.2 accuracy points, or 0.006 JFI are within noise. “First round to reach threshold” is a highly unstable metric.

49: Experimental Design

50: 13. Incomplete ablation. Only aggregation and cohort sizing are ablated. The effects of the KL utility, fairness term, uncertainty penalty, and α-control are not isolated, nor compared against random selection. In Table 2, FedFACS-A1 (the core selection mechanism) is slower than Zhai et al. on CIFAR-10 (47/69/117 vs. 44/62/113) and MNIST-S2 (123 vs. 116), so the gains come largely from auxiliary components.

51: 14. Baseline coverage and provenance. Oort, FedCor, q-FFL/AFL, SCAFFOLD, and FedNova are discussed but not evaluated. It is unclear whether baselines were re-run under the same model/setup or copied from the original papers (which used different architectures). Main baseline accuracies are shown only in a radar chart with no numeric table.

52: 15. Fairness evaluation is narrow. JFI is normalized by a client-quality term q_i that depends on the noise level, so part of the “fairness” is simply preferring cleaner clients. The JFI interpretation threshold (0.5) is unfounded, since the minimum is 1/n. Client-level performance fairness (accuracy variance, worst-client accuracy) is not measured. The fairness claim relative to DivFL has no supporting fairness data. In Table 6, FedFACS is below FedFairCS in 2 of 6 settings, and the margins elsewhere are 0.09–0.36 points.

53: 16. Scale and sensitivity. Only 30–100 clients are used, with no scalability, runtime, or memory analysis, and no sensitivity analysis for β, λ, μ, γ, B, C_kmin, C_kmax. The evaluation protocol is vague (test vs. validation set; final vs. best vs. average accuracy; stopping criterion in Configuration C; noise type in S3/S4).

54: 17. Security and trust. Fitness and stability scores are self-reported by clients without verification, which makes the scheme easily manipulable. Deferring adversaries to future work is insufficient given that selection and weighting are built on these values.

55: 18. No theoretical analysis. No convergence guarantee or fairness bound is provided, despite the use of control-theoretic language.
