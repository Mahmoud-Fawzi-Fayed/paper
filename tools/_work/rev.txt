Reviwer 1;
Dear Authors, I recommend Reject for the present submission to the Journal of King Saud University Computer and Information Sciences. The manuscript addresses a relevant problem: balancing predictive performance, client participation fairness, and communication requirements in federated learning under heterogeneous data distributions. Combining client utility, participation history, uncertainty penalties, adaptive cohort sizing, and aggregation weights is potentially useful, and the evaluation across four datasets provides a reasonable starting point. However, the current submission contains fundamental inconsistencies in the fairness mechanism, control update, aggregation rule, and interpretation of communication efficiency. The experimental reporting also leaves substantial uncertainty about implementation correctness, reproducibility, and the significance of the reported improvements. These concerns affect the central contribution rather than only its presentation.
My first concern is that the stated fairness mechanism does not implement the behavior described in the manuscript. Equation (7) resets the idle-round counter to zero when a client is selected but leaves it unchanged when the client is not selected. Consequently, with zero initialization, every counter remains zero throughout training, and Equation (6) gives the same fairness value of one for every client. This contradicts the repeated explanation that fairness priority increases as a client remains unselected. Please establish whether the implementation increments the counter for an unselected client and whether Equation (7) is a transcription error. The counter initialization, update timing, and calculation of the population average must be specified consistently. This distinction is essential because the reported fairness improvements cannot presently be attributed to the mechanism as written.
The adaptive control law in Equation (8) is also not well-defined over the stated operating conditions. Its denominator contains the sum of a fairness regularization term and the signed quantity \(\nu^t(\Delta KL_i^t-F_i^t)\), which can be zero or arbitrarily close to zero. Projection onto \([0,1]\) after evaluating the fraction does not resolve an undefined division. Furthermore, the explanation that a positive deviation signal increases the utility weight does not follow generally from this equation. When the utility term is smaller than the fairness term, a positive deviation can instead decrease the utility weight. Please define the unindexed \(F\), establish the admissible parameter and signal ranges, and demonstrate the intended directional behavior. Describing the mechanism as Lyapunov-inspired does not establish stability, and bounding one mixing coefficient does not establish stable global training. Either provide an appropriate analysis or present the controller as a heuristic with explicitly tested limitations.
The stability-weighted aggregation requires substantive correction or clarification. Equation (13) permits negative stability scores because the variance penalty can exceed the KL-divergence term. Normalizing these scores by their sum in Equation (18) does not ensure nonnegative aggregation weights, and the denominator can vanish through cancellation or when all scores are zero. The latter case is particularly relevant when local and global predictions coincide. Moreover, Equation (4) and Algorithm 2 use unnormalized stability scores, whereas Equations (17) and (18) describe normalized weights. These are different aggregation procedures. Please identify the implemented procedure and define its behavior for negative scores, a nearly zero denominator, and identical client models. Any positivity transformation, regularization, or fallback aggregation rule must be disclosed and evaluated rather than treated as an inconsequential implementation detail.
The interpretation of client utility is not supported by its mathematical definition. The abstract describes an expected reduction in KL divergence, but Equation (5) defines a divergence between local and global predictions, not a before-and-after reduction or an expectation of global improvement. A large disagreement does not, by itself, establish that incorporating the corresponding update will improve the global model. Please distinguish predictive disagreement from beneficial contribution and explain precisely how the divergence is computed for a classification task, including the class dimension, averaging over examples, evaluation batches, and timing relative to local training. The claim that selecting clients with larger divergence reduces inconsistency and improves generalization requires direct evidence. An informative validation would compare the proposed utility scores with the actual held-out loss changes produced by incorporating the corresponding updates.
The uncertainty penalty needs a similarly careful interpretation. Equation (12) measures variation in KL divergence across mini-batches. Such variation does not distinguish noisy data from legitimate heterogeneity, rare classes, or differences in mini-batch composition. Conversely, a consistently misleading client can have low between-batch variance. Please test whether the penalty discriminates corrupted updates from useful but heterogeneous updates, rather than assuming that low variance establishes reliability. The manuscript should disclose the number of scoring batches, their sampling procedure, and the handling of clients with fewer than two batches, for which the displayed sample-variance formula is undefined. Sensitivity to batch size and the penalty coefficient is necessary because both selection and aggregation depend on this quantity.
The dynamic cohort rule is not sufficiently specified to guarantee a valid number of selected clients. Equation (15) is described as producing a convergence efficiency in \([0,1]\), but it becomes negative when validation loss increases and is undefined when the previous loss is zero. Substituting a negative value into Equation (16) produces a cohort larger than the stated maximum. Equation (16) also generally returns a noninteger value. Please specify clipping, denominator safeguards, integer rounding, initialization of the loss history, and handling of insufficient available clients. The rounding convention is especially important when the allowable cohort range is narrow, because small changes in the loss ratio can otherwise result in materially different participation counts. The rule is a heuristic interpolation; the manuscript does not establish that it identifies an optimal or minimum necessary cohort size.
The reference convergence trajectory introduces an important dependency that is not accounted for adequately. Equation (11) obtains the reference rate from a prior run of a baseline or state-of-the-art algorithm under identical experimental conditions. Please identify which method supplies this trajectory in each experiment, how its run and parameters are selected, and how the acceleration index is set. The prior run represents additional calibration work and potentially additional federated communication, which should be reported separately rather than omitted from the efficiency discussion. It must also be clear that test-set information is not used to construct or select the reference. An ablation using an entirely online reference would help establish whether the reported benefit depends on access to a previously completed training trajectory.
The principal communication-efficiency claim is not established by the reported participation counts. According to the architecture and Algorithms 1 and 2, all candidate clients receive the global model, perform local training, calculate scores, and report those scores before the server selects the clients whose model updates will be uploaded and aggregated. Thus, the proposed mechanism selects uploads and aggregation contributions after local training; it does not necessarily reduce the number of clients that download the model or perform computation. Counting only aggregated client updates excludes potentially substantial downlink, scoring, and discarded-training costs. Please distinguish candidate clients, locally trained clients, uploading clients, and aggregated clients, and report total transmitted bytes, local computation, scoring overhead, and elapsed time under an explicit communication model. Fewer uploaded updates can be a useful result, but it is not sufficient evidence of lower overall communication or training cost.
The fairness metric is interpreted incorrectly. Equation (24) applies Jain’s index to the quality-adjusted quantities \(p_i/q_i\). Therefore, an index of one corresponds to equal participation relative to quality, meaning \(p_i\) is proportional to \(q_i\); it does not generally correspond to equal raw participation. This also undermines the later argument that avoiding an index of one avoids forced uniform participation. Furthermore, an index between 0.5 and one does not establish that every client participates. For example, with equal client quality, uniformly serving 90 of 100 clients while never serving the remaining ten gives an index of 0.9. Please define the intended fairness objective consistently and distinguish participation equality, quality-proportional participation, and fairness of predictive outcomes. The proposed interpretation of 0.5 as a fairness boundary also requires justification.
The claims of preventing client starvation and preserving model fairness exceed the supplied evidence. Even with a corrected idle-round counter, the utility–fairness coefficient can reach one and remove the fairness term from the priority score. Moreover, selecting a client does not ensure that it meaningfully influences the model when its aggregation weight can be negligible or negative. Please report selection-frequency distributions, the proportion of clients never selected, maximum waiting times, and cumulative aggregation influence. Per-client or worst-performing-client predictive results would also help determine whether participation improvements translate into more equitable model performance. Without these analyses or a formal guarantee, the manuscript should describe empirical changes in the specified participation metric rather than claiming that starvation and model bias are prevented.
The relationship between the proposed mechanism and the stated optimization problem remains unclear. Equation (1) defines a sample-size-weighted global objective, but the aggregation rule replaces sample-size weights with stability-based weights without explaining the resulting optimization target. Equation (3) is also not a complete subset-selection formulation: the client index remains free, the contributions are not explicitly combined over the selected subset, and the relationship between the objective, cardinality constraints, and communication cost is not established. Please distinguish the learning objective from the client-ranking heuristic and clarify whether the method is intended to optimize the original population objective or a different quality-weighted objective. Claims of optimal selection should be limited to properties that follow from the actual formulation.
The novelty discussion needs to identify a more precise contribution than the integration of several adaptive mechanisms. Your own related-work section describes adaptive client selection, variable participation, fairness-aware scheduling, and contribution-dependent aggregation. However, the manuscript does not establish clearly what new principle or demonstrated capability distinguishes FedFACS from combinations of those ideas. Please compare the proposed mechanisms directly with the closest approaches already discussed, particularly the adaptive selection and aggregation method, AHIBCS, and FedFairCS. The distinction should concern the actual decision rules, required information, computational costs, and experimentally demonstrated trade-offs. Broad statements that existing methods optimize accuracy, fairness, or communication separately are not adequately reconciled with the capabilities attributed to them elsewhere in the literature review.
The ablation study does not isolate several of the manuscript’s central contributions. FedFACS-A1 removes adaptive aggregation and cohort sizing, while FedFACS-A2 retains cohort adaptation but removes adaptive aggregation. These comparisons do not independently establish the benefits of KL-based utility, the idle-round fairness term, the uncertainty penalty, the adaptive controller, or the baseline-derived convergence reference. Please include targeted ablations that replace adaptive mixing with a fixed coefficient, remove the uncertainty penalty, remove the fairness term, and replace the proposed utility with a simple alternative under comparable budgets. Fairness outcomes should be included in these ablations, not only accuracy and rounds to target. The existing results also do not support the claim that every component improves every outcome; for example, Table 2 shows that the full method requires more rounds than FedFACS-A1 for FMNIST under Scenario 1 with \(\alpha=0.5\).
The baseline comparisons require clearer provenance and tighter experimental control. Please state explicitly whether every reported baseline result was reproduced in your implementation or obtained from the corresponding publication. Following a published configuration does not establish that results are directly comparable when architectures, preprocessing, data partitions, initialization, or evaluation procedures differ. This is particularly important because the experiments are divided into three configurations and several discussions refer directly to results from earlier studies. All methods should have clearly documented access to candidate clients, validation information, and any client-quality metadata, with their respective selection and training costs accounted for. The baseline naming also needs correction: the Configuration B figures and tables identify Pow-D, whereas the accompanying accuracy discussion refers to FedProx.
The reported improvements need statistical support. The manuscript does not state the number of independent runs or provide uncertainty estimates for accuracy, fairness, convergence rounds, or participation counts. Several reported differences are small enough that variation in data partitioning, model initialization, local training, or client selection could affect the ranking. Please evaluate multiple independent partitions and random seeds, report means and uncertainty intervals, and use paired comparisons where methods share the same experimental realization. Clarify whether reported accuracy is the final-round value, the best observed value, or an average over rounds. Averaging percentage improvements across datasets does not establish that the improvement is reproducible within any individual dataset–scenario condition.
Important implementation details are missing. The experimental section describes a partially frozen ResNet-18 but does not specify the pretraining checkpoint, image preprocessing, grayscale-channel treatment, normalization, augmentation, or handling of batch-normalization statistics. Please provide complete values and initialization rules for the fairness regularization coefficient, uncertainty penalty, acceleration index, mixing coefficient, scoring-batch count, and cohort bounds in every configuration. The Dirichlet partitioning procedure should include its minimum-client-sample rule, while the noisy-data scenarios should specify the corruption process and randomization. It should also be stated whether the quality information used in Equation (25) is available only for evaluation or is supplied to any competing selection method.
The COVID-19 experiment requires additional dataset and evaluation documentation. The dataset is described as having three classes, whereas the model description states that the final layer is adjusted for ten classes. Please reconcile these statements and report the exact dataset version, selected classes, sample counts, and training, validation, and test partitions. Describe any patient-level grouping, duplicate checks, and source separation used to prevent overlap between evaluation partitions. The manuscript should also identify whether recall and F1-score are macro-averaged, micro-averaged, support-weighted, or calculated for a particular class. Aggregate results alone do not support the broader medical-imaging reliability statements, particularly because Table 4 reports lower recall for FedFACS than AHIBCS on this dataset.
The participation counts need to be reconciled with the configuration-specific cohort rules. Table 5 reports 266 aggregated participations over 35 rounds for FMNIST, giving an average cohort of 7.6, and 1,047 participations over 103 rounds for COVID-19, giving an average of approximately 10.17. The explicitly stated dynamic bounds of eight and ten appear in the Configuration A ablation, so these Configuration B results are not necessarily impossible; however, the applicable bounds are not supplied. Please disclose them and provide per-round cohort logs so that the totals can be independently checked. The manuscript should also distinguish fewer clients per round from fewer cumulative participations caused by reaching a target in fewer rounds, rather than treating these as interchangeable explanations.
The results discussion should represent the observed trade-offs more accurately. In Table 5, FedFACS requires 35 rounds on FMNIST compared with 27 for AHIBCS, approximately 29.6% more rounds, while reducing aggregated participations from 270 to 266. This is a substantial increase in rounds for a small reduction in uploads, not an unqualified convergence improvement. Table 3 also shows that DivFL reaches the MNIST targets faster under both Scenario 1 settings. Figure 10 reports lower fairness for FedFACS on FMNIST in Scenario 3, and Table 6 contains conditions where FedFACS does not achieve the highest accuracy. Please discuss these results explicitly and explain the operating conditions under which the proposed trade-off is advantageous. The conclusion should not imply simultaneous superiority in accuracy, fairness, and convergence across all evaluated settings.
Several reporting inconsistencies should be corrected through a systematic audit. The FMNIST Scenario 2 accuracy is 83.80% in Table 1 but 83.08% in the accompanying discussion. Figure 9 labels gains as relative percentages while presenting differences that correspond to percentage points; for example, increasing accuracy from 53.1% to 54.3% is a gain of 1.2 percentage points, or approximately 2.26% relative improvement. The round-to-accuracy protocol also needs clarification because the target is initially described through the maximum accuracy attained by FedAvg and later through specified thresholds. Please explain whether entries of exactly 200 rounds indicate actual target attainment or exhaustion of the training budget. Unattained targets should be reported as such rather than assigned a successful convergence time.
Finally, the algorithms should be rewritten to provide one consistent, executable account of the method. The client procedure should explicitly state whether local parameters are reset to the received global parameters at every round, when scoring predictions are computed, and when counters are updated. The server procedure should identify all received information and use the same aggregation formula as the mathematical section. A reproducibility package containing configuration files, partition identifiers, seeds, per-round scores, cohort selections, aggregation weights, and metric outputs would make the central claims auditable. Privacy statements should be restricted to the demonstrated property that raw training data remain local unless an additional privacy analysis is supplied. Claims of scalability and practical deployment should likewise be limited to the synchronous simulated setting unless supported by measurements under increasing client populations and realistic participation constraints.
Reviewer 2
Reviewer Comments
Manuscript: FedFACS: A Fair Adaptive Client Selection Framework for Communication-Efficient Federated Learning
Recommendation: Reject in current form (encourage resubmission after substantial revision)
Summary
The paper proposes FedFACS, a client selection scheme combining a KL-divergence utility, an idle-round fairness term, and a variance-based uncertainty penalty. It adds convergence-driven cohort sizing and stability-weighted aggregation. The idea is reasonable, but the gains are small, the evidence is thin, and several formulations and numbers are inconsistent.
Major Concerns
1. Hidden communication and computation cost. According to Algorithms 1–2, the server broadcasts the model to all clients, and all clients train for E epochs and compute KL and fitness. Only the upload is reduced. Baselines such as FedAvg train only the sampled clients, and Pow-D's probing cost is not counted. Downlink and local-compute costs must be reported; otherwise the “communication-efficient” claim is misleading and the comparison is unfair.
2. Dependence on a baseline run (Eq. 11). The reference rate Ω^t = γ·ρ_base comes from a prior run of a state-of-the-art method, which is unavailable in practice. The controller is effectively tuned to the curve of the competitor it is later compared against. γ is undefined and neither tuned nor analyzed.
3. Incorrect update rule (Eq. 7). An unselected client's idle counter stays at r_i^t and never increases. This contradicts the text, under which fairness grows as a client waits. The fairness mechanism as written cannot work.
4. Ill-posed control law (Eq. 8). The denominator λF + ν(ΔKL − F) can reach zero or become negative. The claimed direction (ν > 0 ⇒ larger α) does not hold in general, since F ≥ 1 usually exceeds ΔKL. λ is never specified. Calling it “Lyapunov-inspired” is unjustified without a Lyapunov function or a stability proof; as written it is a smoothed heuristic.
5. Scale mismatch in Eq. 9. The fairness term F ≥ 1 is unbounded, while ΔKL is typically ≪ 1. Fairness will dominate regardless of α, and the “+1 for numerical stability” does not justify this design.
6. Questionable utility definition. Post-training KL between local and global predictions measures client drift, which under non-IID data is typically harmful (the motivation for FedProx/FedNova). No theoretical or empirical evidence shows that high KL means high utility. The sum over x ∈ X is also ill-defined.
7. Aggregation weights (Eqs. 4, 13, 17–18). s = ΔKL − μσ² can be negative, yielding negative aggregation weights. Dataset size n_i is dropped, which is inconsistent with the objective in Eq. 1. Eq. 4 is unnormalized while Eq. 17 is normalized.
8. Inconsistent time indexing and bounds. ν^{t+1} = Ω^{t+1} − ρ^{t+1} requires future losses. Eqs. 10 and 15–16 mix ρ^t and ρ^{t−1}. If ρ < 0, then (1 − ρ) > 1 and C_k^t exceeds C_kmax. Rounding of the cohort size and the treatment of rounds 1–2 are unspecified. Notation (N/K/C) is inconsistent.
9. Negligible dynamic range of the cohort. The cohort varies only within 8–10 clients (C_kmin = 8, C_kmax = 10), so the claimed communication savings are inherently small. The reported “≈15% fewer clients per round” (Ablation 2) is impossible with these bounds (maximum 20%) and inconsistent with Fig. 4 and Table 3, where average cohort sizes are ≈9.6–9.97.
10. Numerical inconsistencies in the experiments.
•       Table 5: FedFACS on COVID-19 uses 1047 clients over 103 rounds (≈10.17 per round), exceeding the stated cap of m = 10. On FMNIST, 266/35 ≈ 7.6 is below C_kmin = 8. The Configuration B bounds are not reported.
•       Table 4: on balanced test sets (CIFAR-10, FMNIST), macro-recall should equal accuracy. Recall for CIFAR-10 is ≈4 points below accuracy, and F1 exceeds recall by 4–10 points in all rows. For CIFAR-10 (accuracy 54.3%) and FMNIST, the reported F1 values appear arithmetically implausible for macro-averages. The averaging method must be stated and the raw confusion matrices provided.
•       The text reports FMNIST-S2 accuracy of 83.08%, but Table 1 gives 83.80. In that cell the ablated FedFACS-A1 (83.81) is better than the full model.
•       The text names “Random, FedProx and AHIBCS” in the Configuration B comparison, but the tables and figures use Pow-D rather than FedProx.
•       The FMNIST-S2 round reduction is stated as 2.2%, but (108 − 105)/108 ≈ 2.8%.
•       The CIFAR-10 accuracy for Random differs between Fig. 8 and Fig. 9 (51.1 vs. 51.3).
•       Accuracy of ≈47–54% on CIFAR-10 is unusually low for a fine-tuned ResNet-18, and the “fine-tuned for 10 classes” description conflicts with the three-class COVID-19 dataset.
11. Misleading aggregate claims in the abstract. The 0.44% recall “improvement” averages +1.64, +1.15 and −1.48 (FedFACS is worse than AHIBCS on COVID-19 recall). Relative and absolute gains are mixed (e.g., “1.2% relative” is actually 1.2 absolute points; “0.5%” on COVID-19 is absolute). The “best performing existing method” differs by configuration (Zhai, AHIBCS, FedFairCS) and these are never compared in one common setting.
12. No statistical rigor. Results appear to come from a single seed with no standard deviations or confidence intervals. The accuracy curves in Fig. 5 fluctuate by ±4–5 points, so differences of 1–3 rounds, 0.3–1.2 accuracy points, or 0.006 JFI are within noise. “First round to reach threshold” is a highly unstable metric.
Experimental Design
13. Incomplete ablation. Only aggregation and cohort sizing are ablated. The effects of the KL utility, fairness term, uncertainty penalty, and α-control are not isolated, nor compared against random selection. In Table 2, FedFACS-A1 (the core selection mechanism) is slower than Zhai et al. on CIFAR-10 (47/69/117 vs. 44/62/113) and MNIST-S2 (123 vs. 116), so the gains come largely from auxiliary components.
14. Baseline coverage and provenance. Oort, FedCor, q-FFL/AFL, SCAFFOLD, and FedNova are discussed but not evaluated. It is unclear whether baselines were re-run under the same model/setup or copied from the original papers (which used different architectures). Main baseline accuracies are shown only in a radar chart with no numeric table.
15. Fairness evaluation is narrow. JFI is normalized by a client-quality term q_i that depends on the noise level, so part of the “fairness” is simply preferring cleaner clients. The JFI interpretation threshold (0.5) is unfounded, since the minimum is 1/n. Client-level performance fairness (accuracy variance, worst-client accuracy) is not measured. The fairness claim relative to DivFL has no supporting fairness data. In Table 6, FedFACS is below FedFairCS in 2 of 6 settings, and the margins elsewhere are 0.09–0.36 points.
16. Scale and sensitivity. Only 30–100 clients are used, with no scalability, runtime, or memory analysis, and no sensitivity analysis for β, λ, μ, γ, B, C_kmin, C_kmax. The evaluation protocol is vague (test vs. validation set; final vs. best vs. average accuracy; stopping criterion in Configuration C; noise type in S3/S4).
17. Security and trust. Fitness and stability scores are self-reported by clients without verification, which makes the scheme easily manipulable. Deferring adversaries to future work is insufficient given that selection and weighting are built on these values.
18. No theoretical analysis. No convergence guarantee or fairness bound is provided, despite the use of control-theoretic language.
Minor Issues
19.  The statement “ignores clients with smaller datasets” (Sec. 4.1) conveys the opposite of the intended meaning.
20.  Algorithm 1 is mistitled (it is the client-side procedure), L_local is undefined, inputs (“C ⊂ C”) are garbled, and clip(a, 1, 0) has reversed bounds. Eqs. 1–2 use inconsistent indices (i/j).
21.  References: “He et al. 2021” vs. 2016 in the list; MNIST and FMNIST are not cited in the text; ref. [29] (image pooling) is irrelevant; the market-size statement (“USD 311.4 million”) conflicts with the cited title (“billion”) and relies on a press release; the Lyapunov reference [7] is from power engineering.
22.  The conclusion omits COVID-19 from the datasets listed, and claims such as “ensures fairness” and “each component is crucial” exceed the evidence.
23.  No code, data splits, or seeds are provided; a reproducibility statement is needed.
Requests to Authors
•       Report full numeric tables with mean ± std over at least 5 seeds.
•       Provide logs/code to resolve the inconsistencies in comment 10.
•       Report total downlink, uplink, probing, and local-compute costs under a fair protocol.
•       Remove the dependence on a baseline run, or show results without it.
•       Correct Eqs. 7–8 and show the trajectories of α and the cohort size during training.
•       Add a complete ablation and compare with Oort, FedCor, and fairness-oriented methods in a single common framework.
•       Report client-level performance fairness in addition to JFI.
Confidence
High for the formulation and arithmetic issues; moderate for the F1/recall analysis, since the averaging method used by the authors is not stated
